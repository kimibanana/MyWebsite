AI tools can draft a 1,500-word article on laser hair removal in a minute. It’s tempting to fill your website this way. But medical content is different from most topics, and getting it wrong can hurt both your rankings and your patients’ trust.

## What Google actually says

Google’s guidance comes down to one idea: **it rewards content that is helpful, reliable and made for people**, regardless of whether a human or AI produced it. What it acts against is content made mainly to manipulate rankings, especially lots of pages produced at scale with little added value.

For health topics, Google applies an even stricter lens. Medical information falls under what it calls **“Your Money or Your Life” (YMYL)** topics, where poor information could harm someone. For these pages, Google looks hard for signs of **E-E-A-T**:

- **Experience:** does the content reflect real, first-hand experience treating patients?
- **Expertise:** is it written or reviewed by someone qualified?
- **Authoritativeness:** is the clinic or doctor recognized as a source on this topic?
- **Trustworthiness:** is it accurate, transparent and safe?

Generic AI text usually fails the first test. It can sound authoritative while saying nothing your clinic actually knows.

## Where AI helps on a clinic website

Used well, AI saves time on the parts of content work that don’t need clinical judgment:

- **Research and outlines.** Gathering common patient questions and structuring a page.
- **First drafts of non-clinical sections** such as directions, parking, booking steps and what to bring.
- **Rewriting for readability.** Turning a doctor’s technical notes into plain language patients understand.
- **Translations and summaries,** always checked by a fluent human.
- **Meta titles, descriptions and FAQ ideas.**

## Where AI should not have the final word

- **Medical claims, risks and contraindications.** These must come from or be verified by a qualified clinician.
- **Prices, outcomes and recovery times specific to your clinic.** AI will invent plausible-sounding numbers.
- **Before-and-after descriptions, testimonials or statistics.** Fabricated proof can breach advertising rules and destroy trust.
- **Anything compliance-sensitive.** Health advertising rules from regulators such as the FDA and DOH in the Philippines, or their equivalents abroad, apply to your website as well as your ads.

## A safe workflow for AI-assisted health content

1. **Start with your clinic’s knowledge.** Interview the doctor or treatment coordinator for 15 minutes. What do patients ask? What do they misunderstand? What does your clinic do differently?
2. **Use AI to structure and draft** from that input, not from scratch.
3. **Edit for specificity.** Add your locations, doctors, process, real price ranges and cases.
4. **Medical review.** A named clinician checks every clinical statement and signs off.
5. **Show the review.** Display “Medically reviewed by Dr. [Name], [Credentials], on [Date]” on the page.
6. **Fact-check numbers and sources** and link to reputable references such as health departments and peer-reviewed sources.
7. **Re-review regularly,** at least yearly or when guidelines change.

## Signs your content might be hurting you

- Dozens of near-identical articles published in a short time
- Pages with no author, reviewer or date
- Generic advice that could appear on any clinic’s site
- Claims or statistics nobody at the clinic can source
- Rankings that rose briefly after a content push, then fell after a Google update

## The bottom line

AI is a useful assistant, not a replacement for clinical expertise. The clinics that win use it to publish **fewer, better, doctor-reviewed pages** faster, not to flood their site with generic articles.
